# Belief Check: an AI tool for small dental practices' insurance claims

19 Nov 2026

## At a glance

- B1 Staff would let AI draft claims, with approval: Not enough evidence (online only)
- B2 Small practices would pay $300 a month: Not enough evidence (online only)
- B3 Current software leaves claim drafting to staff: Not enough evidence
- B4 Practices lose meaningful staff time to claims weekly: Not enough evidence
- B5 A new app can submit claims through clearinghouses: Supported
- Option 1: Keep building the full draft and submit tool as planned.
- Option 2: Narrow the tool to the claim work staff still do by hand.
- Option 3: Test further before deciding.

Read on for the evidence, or share the one-page summary.

## How to read this

- Sources were read on 19 Nov 2026. Verified means the page was opened and really says this; it doesn't make it true. Secondary means someone else reporting it. Unverified means it couldn't be traced.
- Online only means a belief that needs real people was checked online only, so it can't be Supported.
- Possibly out of date means older than 6 months for prices and features, 12 months for reviews and trends, 2 years for statistics.

## 1. The question

In your words: "Small dental practices waste hours every week on insurance claims paperwork. I want to build an AI tool that fills in and submits the claims."

The people with the problem are independent US dental practices with 1 to 5 dentists. You would build a web app that reads treatment notes and drafts insurance claims for staff to approve. Stage: a prototype being built.

## 2. Beliefs

| ID | Belief | Wrong if... | How sure | Value if right | Cost if wrong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| B1 (riskiest) | Staff at small dental practices would let an AI tool draft their insurance claims, if a person approves each one before it goes. | A survey of US dental practices finds more than half of respondents distrust AI for billing or claims work, or most practices asked have turned down or stopped using an automated claims tool. | Low | High: it is the product's core action | High: the main feature would go unused |
| B2 (riskiest) | Small practices would pay $300 a month for a tool that drafts and submits their claims. | Most small practices spend under $300 a month on claims tools and outsourced billing today, or most comparable claims tools charge under $300 a month. | Medium | High: the only income | High: set by you |
| B3 (riskiest) | Small dental practices still draft claims by hand, because their current software leaves that work to staff. | 3 or more of the leading dental practice software or claims tools already draft claims from clinical notes for staff to approve. | Medium | Medium: room to stand out | High: you would compete with what they already own |
| B4 (sinks the idea if wrong) | Small US dental practices lose meaningful staff time each week to insurance claims paperwork. | Sources that measure it show a typical small practice spends under 5 hours of staff time a week on claims (the AI's guess, which you kept). | High | High: it is the reason anyone would buy | High: no problem means no product |
| B5 | A new app can submit dental claims through a clearinghouse with tools available today. | None of the large US dental clearinghouses offers an API or partner programme open to new software makers. | Medium | Medium: submission saves the last step | Medium: staff could still copy drafts into their own software |

B1, B2 and B3 are the riskiest. B1 and B2 got extra checks, because they matter most to the business if right. A clearinghouse is a company that passes claims from practices to insurers. An API is a way for one piece of software to talk to another.

## 3. What we found

### B1 Staff at small dental practices would let an AI tool draft their insurance claims, if a person approves each one before it goes

**Verdict:** Not enough evidence (online only). Dentists show interest in AI for billing, but no source asked about trusting AI to draft claims.

**Evidence for:**
- Dentists show the most interest in AI for charting, insurance issues and billing (verified, association or professional body, Jul 2026) [S1].
- In a survey of 285 staff in dental and other practices, 41% want to automate billing and payment reminders (verified, news or trade report, undated) [S2].
- 58% of over 160 dental billing professionals have adopted or plan AI and automation in 2026 (verified, vendor's own claim, Feb 2026) [S3].

**Evidence against:**
- In the same survey of 285 practice staff, 51% worry AI will produce low-quality work (verified, news or trade report, undated) [S2].
- In that survey, 60% named patient data privacy as a leading AI risk (verified, news or trade report, undated) [S2].
- About 30% of dentists neither use AI nor plan to (estimate, AI's own arithmetic on [S1]).

**Extra checks:** Checked a second time with different searches, and for one-sided evidence. Nothing new met the bar either way, so the verdict stayed the same.

### B2 Small practices would pay $300 a month for a tool that drafts and submits their claims

**Verdict:** Not enough evidence (online only). Services that do this work cost more than $300 a month. No source showed what most small practices spend or would pay.

**Evidence for:**
- Wisdom charges $1,397 a month for insurance billing under $40,000 a month in collections (verified, vendor's own claim, undated) [S4].
- Dental ClaimSupport's insurance billing, including claim submission, starts at $1,400 a month (verified, vendor's own claim, undated) [S5].
- Dental software buyers budgeted about $688 a month on average (verified, independent data, Jul 2024, possibly out of date) [S6].
- And 3 more in Sources.

**Evidence against:**
- Clearinghouses charge about $0.25 to $0.45 a claim, or $99 a month for unlimited claims (verified, one person's account, undated, possibly out of date) [S7].
- Ready-made insurance automation tools cost $150 to $700 a month per location (verified, one person's account, Oct 2025, possibly out of date) [S8].

**Extra checks:** Checked a second time with different searches, and for one-sided evidence. It found practices already pay $499 to $599 a month for one AI X-ray tool. This didn't change the verdict.

### B3 Small dental practices still draft claims by hand, because their current software leaves that work to staff

**Verdict:** Not enough evidence. Several AI tools now write claim narratives or pick X-rays. Only one says it works from clinical notes, and that is its own claim.

A narrative is the short written explanation an insurer asks for to justify a treatment.

**Evidence for:**
- The endodontists' association says writing a narrative for each claim takes time (verified, association or professional body, Aug 2026) [S12].
- The same association says ready-made narrative templates often lead to denied claims (verified, association or professional body, Aug 2026) [S12].
- A billing company co-founder writes that billers often chase missing details in the chart (verified, one person's account, Aug 2025, possibly out of date) [S13].

**Evidence against:**
- Overjet says its AI reads clinical notes and X-rays, adds procedure codes and builds claims (verified, vendor's own claim, Aug 2026) [S14].
- VideaHealth's tool writes claim narratives and gathers X-ray attachments, including for solo practices (verified, news or trade report, undated) [S15].
- Open Dental's software creates a claim for every finished procedure not yet billed (verified, vendor's own claim, Jun 2025, possibly out of date) [S16].
- And 1 more in Sources.

### B4 Small US dental practices lose meaningful staff time each week to insurance claims paperwork

**Verdict:** Not enough evidence. Industry bodies say claims work costs practices staff time. No source measured the hours for small practices.

**Evidence for:**
- Dental admin transactions cost about $6.6 billion a year, and practices carry most of the labour costs (secondary, news or trade report, Jul 2026) [S18].
- The use of electronic transactions in dentistry slowed or fell in all but one type (verified, association or professional body, 2026) [S21].
- The endodontists' association says writing a narrative for each claim takes time (verified, association or professional body, Aug 2026) [S12].
- And 2 more in Sources.

**Evidence against:**
- Only 0.8% of 751 dentists named time on admin tasks as a top challenge for 2026 (secondary, news or trade report, Jan 2026) [S20].
- Dentistry was credited with more automation in claim submission (secondary, news or trade report, Jul 2026) [S18].

### B5 A new app can submit dental claims through a clearinghouse with tools available today

**Verdict:** Supported. Three clearinghouses publish dental claim APIs, and two outside write-ups confirm it. Searches against found limits, not closed doors.

**Evidence for:**
- Stedi's dental claims API sends claims to hundreds of insurers (verified, vendor's own claim, Nov 2026) [S23].
- DentalXChange offers claim submission and attachments through an API, with access by request (verified, vendor's own claim, undated) [S24].
- Planet DDS, a practice software maker, was the first partner to use DentalXChange's claims API (verified, news or trade report, undated, possibly out of date) [S25].
- And 2 more in Sources.

**Evidence against:**
- Stedi says dental insurers usually need attachments, and it supports them for only some insurers (verified, vendor's own claim, undated) [S28].
- Stedi blocks electronic attachments for insurers that don't accept them, Cigna for example (verified, vendor's own claim, Nov 2026) [S29].

## 4. Not tested with real people

- B1: Whether staff would let AI draft claims depends on why people act. Surveys show interest and worries, but nobody was asked about drafting claims with approval.
- B2: What practices would pay depends on what they spend now and value. The share of small practices that outsource billing wasn't found, so what most spend today is still open.

## 5. What this could mean

### Option 1: Keep building the full draft and submit tool as planned

**Gains:** Clearinghouses publish dental claim APIs today, so submitting from a new app is possible.

**Costs:** At least three funded tools already write narratives or build claims, and trust and price are untested.

**Rests on:** B5 Supported (based on three clearinghouses' own pages and two outside write-ups).

### Option 2: Narrow the tool to the claim work staff still do by hand

**Gains:** Practice software already creates the claim form from finished procedures. Sources point to narratives and attachments as work left to staff.

**Costs:** Overjet, VideaHealth and Pearl already offer narratives or attachments, and attachment support varies by insurer.

**Rests on:** B3 Not enough evidence (weak: one association and one billing company's account).

### Option 3: Test further before deciding

**Gains:** Talking to staff about their last claims would cover time spent, trust, today's costs and tools tried.

**Costs:** It takes weeks of interviews, and building may slow while you wait for answers.

**Rests on:** B1 and B2 Not enough evidence (online only), and B4 Not enough evidence.

> Choosing between these is a judgement call about your product: your goals, your users and what you're willing to trade off. It's often worth talking it through with someone who has made calls like this before.

## 6. Honest limits

- No source measured weekly staff hours on claims for small practices. This is why B4 is Not enough evidence.
- No real people were asked. This is why B1 and B2 are online only and can't be Supported.
- Most price figures come from companies selling billing services, or their blogs. This weakens B2's evidence on both sides.
- No source said what share of small practices outsource billing. This is why the first half of B2's "wrong if" couldn't be checked.
- Companies that tried and stopped weren't researched in depth, and none in dentistry was found. No verdict changed.

This idea touches patient health records, health data law and insurance billing rules. An adviser in those fields should check the findings before anyone acts on them.

How this was done. The research used problem evidence, a scan of competing tools and prices, what practices spend today, technical documents, and a short look at companies that stopped. It ran 28 searches. Searches worded against each belief, with at least one page opened: B1 4, B2 3, B3 3, B4 3, B5 2. B1 and B2 were checked a second time with new search words and new kinds of sources, such as journal studies and review sites. That second check was done by the same AI, not a separate one, because no separate one could be started. It found nothing that changed a verdict. Both were also reread for one-sided evidence and leaning words, and two leaning words were removed. Two searches opened no page. One page was behind a sign-in wall. Neither changed a verdict.
